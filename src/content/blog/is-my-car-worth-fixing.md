There is no single repair-price threshold that tells everyone to replace a car. Start with a confirmed diagnosis and a picture of the vehicle’s overall condition. Then compare repairing the car you know with obtaining and maintaining a replacement that meets your needs.

## Get a reliable repair scope first

Ask what has failed, which tests support the diagnosis, and whether the estimate covers the complete repair. Separate the current problem from maintenance that is due and optional work. An unexplained list makes a repair-or-replace decision harder than it needs to be.

If the recommendation is substantial or uncertain, [request a second opinion on the estimate](/second-opinion/). Paperwork may reveal questions to ask, but an inspection may be necessary before deciding whether the work is justified.

## Look beyond this one repair

A condition assessment can help you understand the rest of the car: brakes, tires, steering and suspension, leaks, and visible structural concerns. Bring service records and tell the shop about recurring problems. Ask which findings affect safe use now and which need planning over time.

An inspection cannot guarantee how long the engine or transmission will last. It can make the decision better informed by identifying observable concerns and the limits of the inspection.

## Compare two realistic options

Write down the repair estimate and other known near-term needs for your current vehicle. Alongside that, write down the actual replacement vehicle you are considering, its purchase and transaction costs, and any immediate work it needs. Include the disruption caused by downtime and whether either option meets your transportation needs.

A repair that costs more than a car’s trade-in offer is not automatically irrational: a suitable replacement may cost much more. Equally, previous spending does not make another major repair worthwhile by itself. The question is what each option offers from today onward.
