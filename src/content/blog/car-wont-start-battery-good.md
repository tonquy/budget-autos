A battery that is new, shows voltage, or powers the lights has not necessarily passed a starting-load test. If the battery has tested good, the next checks depend on whether the engine turns over at all.

## Does the engine crank?

**Nothing happens, or there is a click:** the starting circuit, cable connections, starter, or a start-permission issue may need checking. Describe exactly what you hear instead of ordering a starter from the symptom alone.

**The engine turns slowly:** battery performance under load and resistance in the cables or connections are relevant. Cold conditions can make an existing weakness more apparent.

**The engine turns normally but never starts:** that calls for a different diagnostic path, including fuel delivery, ignition, engine timing, or security-system operation. A jump-start is not a universal fix.

Working headlights do not prove the battery can supply enough current to start the engine. [AAA explains battery and charging-system symptoms](https://www.aaa.com/autorepair/articles/bad-alternator-vs-bad-battery).

## What can I check without taking anything apart?

Once the vehicle is safely parked, check the owner’s manual for the correct starting procedure and any key-not-detected message. For an automatic, confirm it is in Park. Record dashboard warnings and whether the problem began after a battery replacement, repair, long period parked, or a stall.

Avoid repeated long cranking attempts. Do not bypass starter wiring or safety switches. Hybrids and electric vehicles have different starting and high-voltage systems; follow their manuals and leave high-voltage components alone.

## What does the shop need to know?

Send the year, make, model, mileage, where the car is parked, and which of the three starting patterns matches. Include the battery test report if there is one. A short recording made safely from a parked vehicle may help describe the sound, but it cannot prove which component failed.
